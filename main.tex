\begin{abstract}
  Behaviour is a quintessential phenomena in living entities. In this thesis, we deeply explore several facets of human behaviour, starting from the most simple and controlled lab environment, passing through the clinic and ending on an exploration of "behaviour in the wild".

  In the first chapter we adapt a task from rodents to human participants. To do so, we shape it as a videogame. In this game, subjects must accrue evidence about the state of the environment by exploiting it and decide when it's ripeness is undesireable and explore. We find that not only we are able to replicate the rodent behaviour but also that humans learn faster. We also find that the crucial measure of this task is persistent across several visits from the same participant to the lab.

  In the second chapter, we employ a battery of three tasks to a clinical population: a Reversal Learning Task, a Stop-Signal Reaction Time task and the Foraging Task from chapter 1. Participants with a diagnosis of Obsessive Compulsive Disorder were recruited together with matched healthy controls for this experiment. We found that none of the proposed tasks were sensitive to our population. Nonetheless, we found that all the results seem to trend in the direction we hypothesized based on previous literature.

  In the third and final chapter, we tackle more complex behaviour. Intelligence can be viewed as the ability to efficiently find effective solutions within a large space of possibilities. To identify the unique ingredients of human intelligence, we need cognitive tasks that are sufficiently complex to challenge intelligent agents while being simple enough to yield generalizable models of intelligent behavior. We developed such a task in the form of a video game that has been played by over 10,000 users. We find three ingredients that distinguish human learning – humans are 1) “picky” - they use a highly restricted subset of the available action sequences; 2) “sticky” – they attempt even unrewarding solutions repeatedly before discarding them; “leapy” - arrive at the optimal policy suddenly and unpredictably with a “leap of insight”. Our data suggest a “top-down” learning process by which humans converge on common explanatory solutions which they replace only upon collecting sufficient evidence to the contrary.

\end{abstract}

\section*{Thesis Overview}
This thesis is divided into three chapters, each delving further away from the wet bench and into the wild.

In the first chapter, a simple task is adapted from a rodent experiment into humans.

In the second, a battery of tasks was ran on a population constituted of healthy and Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD) patients, looking at differences across these two groups.

In the third and final chapter, a task was developed and launched as a videogame which thousands of participants attempted to solve - their complex behaviours here described in detail.

This thesis is an exploration in human behaviour and what behaviours emerge when they (we) are faced with carefully designed task environments.

\section{General Introduction}
\subsection{Introduction}
Human behaviour has long been one of the most studied phenomena in nature. Even before the dawn of psychophysics in the 1860s \cite{fechner1948elements}, there have been countless studies and even fields of research attempting to make sense of what and how we do things. These efforts are often focused on specific tasks: on the one extreme, tasks developed to study specific behaviours (i.e. a forced choice Decision Making task (AFC)), and on the other, more naturalistic tasks like learning how to walk or read. How can we make sense of behaviour is way too broad of a question to focus on. Instead, in this work, I will focus on a battery of tasks - from the very simple to the complex, from learning to learned, from the lab to the clinic - and attempt to shine some light on what human behaviour can look like in them.
\subsection{Decision Making and Psychophysics}
One of the prominent fields of research of Behavioral Sciences is that of Decision Making. Here, scientists adventure to model, create and test environments that operate under specific constraints. These task constraints constitute the tools with which we can ask questions about specific behaviours. As an example, a two alternative forced-choice task may ask participants to tell wether a a cloud of moving dots is moving rightwards or leftwards \cite{newsome1989neuronal}. As one approximates a dot motion coherence of 50\%, so should the accuracy of responses diminish. In this very simple case, by looking at the psychometric curve of response accuracy given the stimulus orientation, one can begin to answer the fundamental question of psychophysics: what is the relationship between stimuli and perception.
\subsubsection{How do we make decisions}
After defining our task, the readout of a decision is most often behavioural: a movement, a verbal answer, the press of a button. What leads to these behaviours is one of the most prolific areas of the study of Decision Making.
\paragraph{Neural basis of Decision Making}\label{neural_basis}
A multitude of brain regions are involved in Decision Making. Depending on the task, certain sensory organs and corresponding cortical areas are recruited. Furthermore, the Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC), Orbitofrontal Cortex (OFC) and the Ventromedial Pre-frontal Cortex (VmPFC) are all regions known to be involved in Decision Making. The OFC appears crucial for hidden state representation, and hence for inference-based decisions \cite{rolls2004functions} \cite{vertechi2020inference}. For example, in both primates and rats, the inhibition or lesion of the OFC seems to impair performance in reversal learning tasks \cite{wilson2014orbitofrontal}. The ACC, on the other hand, is thought to be involved in integrating value \cite{williams2004human}. The VmPFC seems critical for the representation of both reward and value \cite{hiser2018multifaceted}.
Most studies thus tend to focus on these (and adjacent) regions as they attempt to highlight the underlying circuitry in Decision Making.
\paragraph{Modeling the problem}
To make sense of both behavioural and neural findings, one can opt to normatively formalize the problem (i.e. model them). Modeling a Decision Making task requires a deep understanding of its environment. Often, when modeling a task, it's crucial to consider what an optimal agent would behave like.

In realistic scenarios, subject behaviour is often sub-optimal. An ideal model is such that, by manipulating its parameters, one is able to replicate both optimal and sub-optimal (i.e. subjects') behaviour. Once again taking our random moving dots task as an example, one can model an optimal agent with a simple sigmoid/logistic function and account for human error by tuning the steepness of its curve.
While such elegant models are a rare sight to behold, the idea of conceptualizing a problem mathematically is one of the most powerful ways one has to make predictions about behaviour and an essential component in most contemporary scientific studies.


\subsubsection{How do we learn to make decisions}
The decision-making process often starts with sensory input from the environment, such as visual, auditory, or tactile information. Sensory receptors collect this data and transmit it to the brain. \cite{mesulam1998sensation}
The brain's sensory cortices (e.g., the visual cortex for visual information) process this raw data into a coherent perceptual experience. This involves integration and interpretation of sensory input to form a meaningful context.
Upon reaching the cortex, the information is evaluated by the prefrontal areas (see \ref{neural_basis}).

 Recruiting the Basal Ganglia and the Dopaminergic system - where action selection and reward prediction errors are computed respectively - our brains then use Reinforcement Learning (RL) mechanisms to adapt and refine Decision Making over time \cite{schultz1997neural}.

Then, memory comes into play. Areas like the hippocampus and the entorhinal cortex contribute to memory consolidation \cite{squire1991medial}.

This description is equal parts incomplete as it is reductionist.

\paragraph{We don’t all learn the same}
However known these neural systems are, there is always variance in observed behaviour. Learning differences may be due to different factors: Genetic, Neurobiological, Emotional, Neuroplasticity, etc. \cite{zatorre2012plasticity} \cite{ericsson1993role}. Learning differences may be one of the explanatory causes for the behavioural variability that is very often observed on any particular task.
Recent discoveries like the complete neural connectome for several animal species, have highlighted what we suspected long ago: there are many a paths through which information flows in the brain \cite{asahina2022editorial}. Conversely, it is increasingly accepted that even the simplest of neural circuits can lead to a variety of behavioural outputs \cite{cruz2022action}.
Simultaneously, environmental changes in behaviour can significantly alter behaviour. As an example, zebrafish alter their turning behaviour based on the temperature of the water in which they grew in \cite{hageter2021environmental}. Social interactions and hierarchies can also change behaviour, as it has been demonstrated across different mammalian species (e.g. in rodents \cite{faure2022social}).

Ultimately - and given your specific genome, neural circuitry, development circumstances, social interactions, etc. - it is by analyzing the current state of the environment and taking into account its pasts states that one learns and decides on how to solve a task.

\paragraph{Inter and Intra-Subject variability}
While it may seem like a trivial affair, distinguishing inter and intra-individual variability requires a large amount of data - and repeated measures - from a given individual. What are individual behavioral differences and what are differences across individuals is a question that is relevant in any study. Big Data approaches have recently been found useful to address this type of problem.

\subsection{Complexity}
As we distance ourselves from discrimination tasks, both the underlying neural mechanisms needed to produce an answer and plausible models of the data become ever more complex. While a simple sigmoid suffices to model behaviour in the random moving dots task and information from neurons in the visual cortex alone is enough to solve it \cite{newsome1989neuronal}, this is not the case for the large majority of tasks. Coming up with tasks that are simple to model and solve and yet complex enough to elicit interesting responses is thus one of the greater challenges in the study of Decision Making.

\subsubsection{What does it mean to be complex}
One definition of complexity is how large the dimensionality of a problems' state-space is. Van Opheusden and Ma (2019), have attempted to quantify a battery of problems in terms of their complexity. In their study, they confirmed something that has long been hinted at: Neuroscience tends to stick to low-complexity problems. In contrast, the field of Artificial Intelligence (AI) - full to the brim with data - tends to focus on extremely complex tasks. This begs the question as to what lives in the middle ground, and why are we not exploring it.
\paragraph{The curse of dimensionality}
With increasingly complex questions, so does the amount of data required to answer them increase. This, in other words, is the problem that Bellman \cite{bellman1957dynamic} highlighted and named \textit{the curse of dimensionality}.
In it, it is stated that, as the dimensionality of a problem increase, data points become ever more sparse, making it difficult to discern meaningful relationships. To thoroughly sample a larger space, exponential amounts of data are required. This is an especially relevant problem in the fields of AI and Machine Learning (ML).

This curse is not without its cure. Reducing the dimensionality of the dataset is the solution scientists have found to deal with the problem. Be-it by manually feature extracting important factors before analysis or by utilizing dimensionality reduction techniques (e.g. Principal Component Analysis (PCA)).
\paragraph{Discerning a signal amongst noise}
This is however easier said than done. Manually extracting the relevant features requires the analyst to make assumptions about the dataset. Determining what is signal and what is noise is an extremely important decision. Dimensionality reduction techniques aren't necessarily an instantaneous solution either: 1) the linear combinations of the original dataset often lead to a loss of data; and 2) it becomes difficult to determine which features are the most significant in the dataset.

\subsubsection{Why studying complexity matters}
Handling complex datasets seems to be easier said than done, so why is there even an attempt to do so?

Firstly, some problems are inherently complex. In fact, most naturalistic behaviours are. One of the most explored fields is that of language. While heavily theorized on, it was in fact in the last decades that language studies datasets have had a resurgence. Statistical language modelling - or the capability to generate language from observed data - saw it's first steps in the 1980's. Quickly, scientists ran into the curse of dimensionality. However, and synchronously, a new technology grew - the Internet. Developers from Google were quick to understand that this "dataset" was unrivaled and that leveraging it was the way forward. Indeed, in 2018, they launched BERT - a flagship model that learns to represent text as a sequence of vectors probabilistically similar to the previous. Today, with the rise of the GPT-family models, Large Language Models (LLMs) have quickly become ubiquitous. These models rely on dozens of billion parameters and leverage the latest advances in neural network algorithms to generate text in natural language. Natural Language Processing has dramatically been improved and its methods radically altered by the availability of this corpus of data and algorithms \cite{guo2025deepseek}.

Other notable examples of success due to large datasets are that of Stockfish and Dall-e (an algorithm that solves the game of chess and an image generation algorithm respectively). These are, however, not the standard for psychology .

\paragraph{Learning from Psychophysics}
As highlighted before, in psychophysics, simple tasks are boiled down to an (or several) essential measurements that succinctly describe behaviour. This powerful approach allows for comparisons in and across subjects.

Suffice to say, this methodology does not come without some qualms:

1. It is often hard to reduce a behavioural phenomena that may be extremely complex to a single measurement.

2. Psychophysics tasks often require large repetitions of trials in cumbersome settings, which can lead to fatigue.

3. Sensory adaptation can occur through exposition to a task, making measurements unreliable.

4. Individuals may behave in drastically different ways, impeding generalizable measures.

5. Individuals may have different perceptions even when present with the same stimulus, which can lead to radically different answers.

All of these must be taken into account when investigating behaviour and applying these tools. Our ambition is to be rigorous and extend these tools to increasingly complex datasets without falling into its pitfalls.

\paragraph{Extending from Psychophysics}
One way to extend these tools to account for more complex tasks is to start simple. In a game of chess, one might be compelled to understand how to win. Instead, we propose starting with the first move.
This approach comes with several advantages.

1. It maximizes data: all games start on the first move.

2. It ensures comparability across subjects.

3. It ensures the root of the behavioral tree is explored before dwelling into the branches: no assumptions are made about the more complex aspects of the data.

Following this approach, we can slowly navigate the layers of complexity of any given task.

\paragraph{Do we have the tools and the means?}
An approach like the proposed may seem unrealistic, depending on the task. A critic might even say that learning about the first move would lead nowhere, if the goal is to understand chess.
The single most crucial point thus becomes choosing our research question. We must be aware of the limitations of our approach and design/choose tasks that thrive in them.

\paragraph{Modelling complexity}
One of the tools at our disposal is to compare our investigations with models. Starting with the simplest possible - 0th order model - and growing our models in complexity as needed. In a game of chess, we can imagine that the simplest possible model for the first move is to play a random piece. It should be immediately apparent why this approach is not as simple as it seems. All tasks have rules, and accounting for them is needed. One cannot simply move any chess pieces (e.g. bishops don't go through pawns.)
Building the environment first is the needed missing step. We must create an environment to test our models on that follows exactly the rules of our task.
That means our task must be computationally formalized, and while most things are, usually it does not come without its challenges (e.g. the rules of chess alone require between two to three thousand lines of code.)

\subsubsection{In this thesis...}
In this thesis we will be exploring multiple tasks, of differing levels of complexity. While all of them are ran in humans, they all require specific methodologies to analyze. It is because of this that we chose to go from the more simple to the more complex. Beginning with the lab environment and ending in the real world. We hope to have selected the right tools for the job at hand.

\section{Behaviour in the lab}
\subsection{Introduction}
\subsubsection{Decision Making}

Foraging is a fundamental behavior observed across species, involving complex decision-making processes that balance the exploitation of known resources with the exploration of new opportunities. Animals, including humans, must continually assess whether to persist in a given location or shift to another in search of better rewards. This trade-off, often framed as the exploration-exploitation dilemma, has been extensively studied in behavioral ecology, neuroscience, and psychology \cite{cohen2007should, hills2015exploration}.

\paragraph{Inference Based vs. Stimulus Bound Behaviour}

While some decision-making models propose that foraging behavior is driven by direct reinforcement from past rewards , others suggest that individuals use inference-based strategies to estimate the underlying availability of resources \cite{behrens2007learning}.

Recent studies on rodent foraging behavior suggest that animals infer hidden states of resource availability rather than merely responding to reinforcement contingencies \cite{vertechi2020inference}. However, it remains unclear whether humans engage in similar inference-based decision-making when faced with analogous foraging challenges. To investigate this, we adapted a rodent foraging task to a human experimental framework, allowing for cross-species comparisons and a deeper understanding of the cognitive mechanisms underlying foraging decisions.

To systematically explore these questions, we conducted two experiments. The first experiment examined how changes in task difficulty influenced foraging behavior by varying reward and transition probabilities. The second experiment investigated the stability of foraging strategies over time by inviting participants for multiple sessions. This allowed us to assess whether human decision-making in this task is consistent across time, an essential consideration for understanding the reliability of foraging strategies \cite{daw2006cortical}.

Our findings contribute to cross-species decision-making by elucidating how humans integrate uncertainty, past experiences, and task constraints when making foraging decisions. Understanding these mechanisms has implications for fields ranging from behavioral economics to artificial intelligence, where optimal decision-making under uncertainty is a crucial challenge \cite{gershman2018deconstructing, sutton1998reinforcement}.

The remainder of this chapter details our experimental design, behavioral results, and a discussion of the implications of our findings for theories of decision-making and foraging behavior.

\subsubsection{Designing experiments across species}

The integration of human and rodent research in neuroscience is crucial for understanding fundamental brain mechanisms and translating findings into clinical applications. Rodents, particularly mice and rats, provide a controlled experimental framework for investigating neural circuits, genetics, and behavior at a level of detail not feasible in human studies. Their well-mapped neuroanatomy and genetic tractability allows researchers to manipulate specific neural pathways and assess causal relationships between brain activity and behavior \cite{gomez2019life}.

However, translating rodent findings to humans requires careful validation, as behavioral and cognitive processes can differ across species \cite{charvet2025translating}. Comparative approaches, such as cross-species tasks assessing decision-making or learning, help bridge this gap by identifying conserved mechanisms while accounting for species-specific adaptations \cite{vertechi2020inference}.

This synergy between rodent and human research is particularly valuable in areas like neuropsychiatric disorders, where preclinical models inform treatment strategies, but human validation remains essential for clinical relevance.

By leveraging both human cognitive complexity and rodent experimental precision, neuroscience can more effectively unravel the principles governing brain function and behavior \cite{krakauer2017neuroscience}.

In an attempt to do so, in this chapter we adapted a rodent foraging task to humans, carefully checking if the same predictions and findings held true across species.


\subsection{Materials and Methods}
\subsubsection{The "Hidden-State" task}
In natural foraging behaviors, animals are faced with a constant dilemma: whether to stay and exploit resources at their present location or , with associated costs, to venture out and explore alternative, potentially more beneficial sites. When viewed through the lens of optimal decision-making, the key question is to determine the most advantageous moment to abandon the current site in favor of the next. To gather insight on this process, a simple behavioral task was developed in mice \cite{vertechi2020inference}. In it, at any given moment, rewards are available at one of two distant foraging sites. For each exploration on the rewarded site, rewards are administered following a probability P(reward). Simultaneously, for each exploration, rewards change site availability following a probability of transition P(transition). Crucially, these are not explicitly available to the agent - they  must be inferred. Rewarded trials occur when foraging on an active site where P(reward) was triggered. Unrewarded trials, however, aren't as clear. One must estimate, for any given trial - taking into account the task history - wether an exploration was unrewarded because rewards are currently available only on another site or wether an exploration was just "unlucky" (i.e. did not trigger P(reward)).
Subjects are thus tasked with inferring a hidden state - reward availability - from a sequence of rewards and failures.

\paragraph{Adapting the task to humans}
To allow for behavioral comparison across-species, the task was adapted to humans. We developed a video-game like task where subjects were instructed to control a character on a touch-screen device. This character was tasked with finding and hitting a "monster". This "hidden-state monster" hid in one of two walls (foraging sites), separated by a costly to travel on path. Hitting the monster is analogous to getting a reward - awarded in game points. The cost of traversal was manipulated by making the path slower or faster to travel across. P(reward) and P(transition) were kept equivalent to the mouse counterpart.

\paragraph{How to solve it} There are several ways in which to integrate rewards and failures towards a decision. In a stimulus-bound process, the value of a site is directly related to the rewards on that site, decreases with rewards on the other side and decays to 0 with failures. An alternative process is that of using inference to solve the task: an agent that is aware of the fact that a hidden state determines reward availability can use rewards and failures differently to determine wether the current site is ripe or not.
Both stimulus bound and inference models predict that the probability of leaving a site increases with failures, however, the way rewards are represented is quite different \cite{vertechi2020inference}. In a stimulus bound process, accruing rewards on a given side would increase the value of said side and thus increase the likelihood of remaining. In an inference based process, rewards reset the probability of leaving a site to 0, as it provides the agent with certainty about the hidden state.

Human participants were invited to participate in this research study. Their participation varied across several experiments.

For a comprehensive comparative study refer to \cite{vertechi2020inference}.

After informed consent, participants were presented with the experimental protocol. In it, they experienced the task a total of 4 times. P(reward) and P(transition) were manipulated in the following protocols:

\subsection{Results}

\subsubsection{Behaviour is maintained across changes in the task}
As demonstrated in \cite{vertechi2020inference}, changes in protocol were reflected in consistent behavioural changes.

This task investigates if participants solve it in an inference-based or stimulus bound fashion. To investigate whether that was the case, we look at reward history. In a stimulus bound process, accruing rewards on a given side would increase the value of said side and thus increase the likelihood of remaining. In an inference based process, rewards reset the probability of leaving a site to 0, as it provides the agent with certainty about the hidden state. In an inference based process, the number of attempts on a given side after the last reward was administered should thus not be reliant on the reward history. \subsection{Discussion}

This first chapter aimed to investigate whether human participants solve a foraging task using an inference-based or stimulus-bound decision-making process. By adapting a behavioral paradigm originally designed for mice, we examined how human participants responded to changes in reward probability, transition probability, and traversal costs. Our results suggest that participants employ an inference-based strategy, adjusting their behavior according to the task demands and maintaining stable performance across different protocols and experimental sessions.

\subsubsection{Inference-based decision-making in human foraging}
The findings align with previous research \cite{vertechi2020inference} demonstrating that humans use inference-based strategies to estimate reward availability. This is evident in the fact that, regardless of the total number of rewards obtained, participants did not increase their persistence on a given site. Instead, their probability of leaving a site was reset after receiving a reward, supporting the notion that rewards serve as confirmatory evidence of the hidden state rather than directly reinforcing a given location. This contrasts with a stimulus-bound strategy, where the value of a location would increase with the accumulation of rewards, leading to site persistence.

As the difficulty of the protocol increased, the number of attempts after the last reward also increased. This further supports the inference-based decision-making framework. Participants required more evidence before switching sites when the task was more uncertain, which suggests they were actively inferring the hidden state rather than simply reacting to immediate rewards and failures in a stimulus-bound fashion.

\subsubsection{Task sensitivity and behavioral adaptation}
The results indicate that human participants adapt flexibly to task changes. The different protocols elicited systematic variations in behavior, with participants making more attempts after the last reward in harder protocols. This aligns with the hypothesis that participants adjust their exploration-exploitation trade-off based on task demands. Moreover, the learning curves suggest that participants rapidly adapted to new conditions, particularly as they transitioned from the medium protocol to either the easy or hard protocols. This highlights the flexibility of human decision-making and the ability to integrate prior experience into ongoing behavior.

The effect of traversal cost on decision-making further supports this notion. When movement between foraging sites was slowed down, participants increased their persistence at a given location before switching. This finding suggests that participants integrate costs into their decision-making process, optimizing their strategy based on both reward probabilities and movement constraints. Such behavior is consistent with an optimal foraging framework, in which organisms seek to maximize reward while minimizing costs.

\subsubsection{Stability of behavior across sessions}
The second experiment clarifies that participants' decision-making strategies remain stable over time. The number of attempts after the last reward remained consistent across sessions, and the intraclass correlation analysis confirmed strong reliability of this measure. The Bland-Altman analysis further suggested that individual differences in behavior were maintained across visits, with minimal systematic bias. However, some heteroscedasticity was observed, indicating that reliability decreased slightly for individuals who had higher values of attempts after the last reward.

This consistency over time underscores the robustness of the inference-based approach and suggests that individuals develop and retain an internal model of task dynamics that guides their decisions across sessions.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}

The consistency of behavior across different conditions suggests that these strategies are flexible and context-dependent. Understanding how humans balance exploration and exploitation has implications for fields such as behavioral economics, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence, particularly in the development of models that seek to mimic human decision-making.

The demonstrated behavioral patterns indicate that decisions are based on inference rather than simple reinforcement learning. Participants flexibly adapted their behavior to task demands and maintained stable decision-making strategies across multiple sessions. These findings contribute to our understanding of human exploration-exploitation trade-offs and offer a framework for future studies on the cognitive mechanisms of decision-making in uncertain environments like the one shown in the following chapter.

\section{Behaviour in the clinic}
\subsection{Introduction}

\subsubsection{Obsessive Compulsive Disorder}

While well characterized psychiatrically, the pathophisiology of Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is one that needs further investigation. In fact, mental disorders as a whole persist as one of the leading contributors to global disease burden, with no significant decline observed in recent decades \cite{gbd2022global}. Among these, Obsessive Compulsive Disorder stands out as a debilitating psychiatric condition characterized by early onset, chronic progression, and frequent comorbidity with both physical and psychiatric comorbidities \cite{katschnig2006quality}. OCD is associated with profound impairments in daily functioning and quality of life, compounded by societal stigma and systemic barriers to care. Despite advances in evidence-based pharmacological and behavioral therapies, treatment resistance and poor adherence remain significant challenges \cite{kellner2010drug}. To address these gaps, novel preventive and therapeutic strategies are urgently needed, requiring deeper insights into the disorder’s underlying mechanisms \cite{m2018promising}.

\subsubsection{Behaviour Matters}
Just like Neuroscience, Psychiatry also needs behaviour \cite{krakauer2017neuroscience}. There has been a notable growth in research of the behavioural underpinnings of psychiatric disorders. A lot of it in reaction to the availability of more and more sophisticated methods of analysis \cite{montague2012computational}. The field of Computational Psychiatry has tackled many a diagnosis in attempts to unravel the computational basis of diagnoses. While it is the case, the adoption of those findings into clinical practice is sparse and not widely regarded as relevant \cite{hitchcock2022computational}.

We argue, however, that without behaviour, we cannot truly understand the computational mechanisms underlying a diagnosis, which leads to a poor understanding and, if not careful, to a poor treatment of said disorder.
Paired with other novel techniques like Functional Magnetic Ressonance Imaging (fMRI), Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS), and many others, careful behaviour analysis remains one of the unexplored dimensions in mental health disorders.
Due to its very characterized phenomena, diagnosis and symptomatology, OCD is a prime candidate disorder to perform this sort of investigation on.

\subsubsection{Obsessive Compulsive Disorder and task performance}
Some authors have expertly tackled this challenge.

When investigating the role of Reversal Learning in OCD, authors find conflicting results. While some report a difference in performance between healthy and OCD patients and in activity in the OFC \cite{remijnse2006reduced}, others find differences only in more subtle metrics and when looking at symptom severity \cite{valerius2008reversal}.

The more commonly used task in OCD research is, however, the Stop Signal Reaction Time Task (SSRTT). In a recent review, authors found that Stop Signal Reaction Time (SSRT) was significantly increased in most studies with patients with an OCD diagnosis \cite{mar2022obsessive}. They did not find significant differences in reaction times across groups. OCD has also been linked with dysfunction in frontal areas of the cortex like the Orbitofrontal Cortex (OFC) and the medial Prefrontal Cortex (mPFC) \cite{aron2003stop}.

When developing the battery of tasks for this experiment, we also considered the "Hidden State Task", as it not only allows for cross-species work with rodents, but also has been found to be sensitive to disruptions in the OFC \cite{vertechi2020inference}.

\subsection{Materials and Methods}
\subsubsection{The experiment}
The work in this chapter was developed under a larger protocol to assess novel diagnostics in Obsessive Compulsive Disorder. For this experiment, three tasks were selected due to their relevance with the clinical sample.

\paragraph{The subjects}
A sample of matched participants for sociodemographic characteristics - age, sex and education -  was collected. A total of 200 participants were recruited (100 healthy and 100 diagnosed with OCD according to DSM-V criteria). Participants were screened in a battery of tests, most relevantly on Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (YBOCS-II) \cite{goodman1991yale}. Patients and controls were included if they were between 18 and 65 years old, were fluent Portuguese speakers and did not meet any of the following exclusion criteria: any acute medical illness, history of substance abuse or dependence (other than nicotine) in the last 12 months, neurodevelopmental disorders, dementia or any form of cognitive impairment, active neurological disease, illiteracy or inability to understand the study’s instructions, and inability to provide informed consent.

For each of the tasks, some participants were excluded, either due to technical issues with the data collection or because they did not meet the inclusion criteria for the larger protocol.

Our clinical cohort consisted of patients diagnosed with Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD) according to the DSM-V criteria. These patients were recruited as a part of a larger comprehensive study on OCD and markers of diagnosis - Neurocomp. This study was approved by the FChampalimaud ethics committee and conducted in the Neuro Psychiatry Unit of the Champalimaud Research.

\subsubsection{The tasks}
For this protocol, three different tasks were selected. Two for historical correlations with OCD and one for it's novelty.
\paragraph{Reversal Learning Task}
Firstly, a classical reversal learning was adapted from the work of \cite{remijnse2006reduced}. Here, a deficit in reversal learning and aberrant OFC-striatal and dorsal prefrontal activity was correlated with the diagnosis of OCD.

In each trial, the task consisted of 2 stimuli images presented at either side of a screen. The images had randomized locations and were shown for a maximum time of 3000 milliseconds. Participants selected one of the images by pressing the right or left keys on a keyboard. On a correct response, rewards - an increase in points - were administered 80 percent of the time. For the remainder 20 or for spontaneous errors (SE), a punishment - decrease in points - was administered. A correct response with a rewarded outcome was named a Correct Response (CR). A response that albeit correct was probabilistically attributed a punishment was named a Probabilistic Error (PE). PE's could lead to either a shift or a non-shift in the choice: Probabilistic Error Shift (PES) or Probabilistic Error No Shift (PENS).

Criterion for the trigger of a reversal happened stochastically after 6 to 10 consecutive correct responses. After reversal, a response that persisted on the pre-reversal stimulus was deemed a Preceding Reversal Error (PRE). The last response before an error was named a Final Reversal Error (FRE).

Trials ended with a 2000 millisecond feedback window where the outcome was displayed, followed by a fixation cross of 1000ms and a subsequent trial.

Subjects were instructed to accrue as many points as possible. Speed was not encouraged.

A neutral baseline trial (BL) was also presented randomly interleaved with the probing trials. This trial consisted of two images of a ball and a pencil. Here subjects were instructed to always select the option "pencil". In these trials, a neutral feedback was given.

Subjects were excluded if they did not meet a certain performance level (a minimum of 5000 points), or if they, on average, answered wrongly on BL trials. A total of 15 subjects (8 healthy and 7 ocd) did not meet the criteria.

\paragraph{Stop-Signal Reaction Time Task}
Secondly, a stop-signal reaction time task (SSRTT) was adapted from \cite{aron2003stop}. In this family of tasks, Participants are asked to make speedy answers on go trials and withhold from responses on no-go trials.
In the SSRTT, most trials are go trials. However, in a fraction of trials, a tone will be played. When the tone is played, participants should withhold their response.

In this version of the task, an arrow to the left or to the right was presented. The order in which right- and left-pointing arrows were presented was randomized to ensure an unbiased distribution. Subjects were instructed to press the correspondent key on a physical keyboard. In 25\% of the trials, chosen at random, the audible stop signal was played shortly after the arrow was shown. The interval between the initial presentation of the arrow and the subsequent stop signal tone is known as the stop-signal delay (SSD).

To keep the task challenging, the inter-stimulus interval (the time between successive stimuli) and the SSD were dynamically adjusted based on each participant's performance. This adjustment aimed to ensure that participants were able to successfully inhibit their responses on half of the stop trials, striking a balance between success and failure rates. This adjustment was done using a staircase-like process.

From the data, we calculated the stop-signal reaction time (SSRT) for each participant. The SSRT represents the processing time required to inhibit a strong motor response. This was determined by subtracting the average SSD from the median reaction time observed in trials without the stop signal. This metric quantifies how quickly and effectively participants can stop themselves from responding when prompted by the stop signal.

\paragraph{The "Hidden-State" task}
Finally, the hidden-state task was used. This task was developed and elaborated on the first chapter of this thesis. Please refer to that chapter for task methodology as it is unchanged. The chosen protocol for this experiment was the "New Protocol" with a probability of reward of 45\% and a probability of transitioning states of 15\% (Table \ref{table_new}).

\subsection{Discussion}

We aimed to investigate behavioral differences between individuals with OCD and healthy controls across three tasks: Reversal Learning, Stop-Signal Reaction Rime, and a novel "hidden-state" task. We assessed their utility in elucidating computational mechanisms underlying OCD. Contrary to our hypotheses, no significant group differences emerged in any of the metrics we evaluated across tasks. These findings raise questions about the sensitivity of these paradigms in capturing OCD-related behavioral phenotypes and highlight challenges in translating experimental tasks to clinical populations.

\subsubsection{Reversal Learning: Reconciling Mixed Findings}

The absence of significant differences in reversal latency, correct responses, or task scores aligns with some prior studies that report subtle or null effects in OCD populations \cite{valerius2008reversal}. However, this contrasts with work linking OCD to reduced reversal learning efficiency and aberrant orbitofrontal-striatal activity \cite{remijnse2006reduced}. Several factors may explain this discrepancy:

    Symptom heterogeneity: OCD is a heterogeneous disorder, and the lack of stratification by symptom dimensions (e.g., contamination vs. symmetry) may have obscured subgroup-specific deficits.

    Task design: The probabilistic punishment structure (20\% punishment on correct trials) might have introduced noise, diluting reversal-related signals. Future studies could employ deterministic reversals or trial-by-trial computational modeling to isolate decision-making processes.

    Compensation mechanisms: Medicated patients or those with chronic OCD may develop compensatory strategies, masking deficits observable in early-stage or untreated cohorts.

\subsubsection{SSRTT: Stopping to reassess}

The SSRTT results mirror recent meta-analysis findings of inconsistent SSRT differences in OCD \cite{mar2022obsessive}. Notably, the dynamic adjustment of stop-signal delays (SSD) failed to plateau in many participants, violating the race model’s assumptions \cite{verbruggen2019consensus}. This suggests that the task’s adaptive algorithm may not have stabilized sufficiently, potentially invalidating SSRT estimates. Even after restricting analyses to participants with plateaued SSDs, group differences remained absent, raising flags about the task’s validity for OCD.

Verbruggen et. al (2019) warn us that some rules are meant to be followed. One of them, the importance of stressing the participants about answering as fast as possible, was not present in our study. What was also not present was a training stage where participants got to learn the mechanics of the task beforehand. These two omissions may have been crucial for the negative results we present here.

\subsubsection{Hidden-State Task: Unexplored Dimensions}

The hidden-state task, designed to probe inference-based decision-making, also failed to differentiate between groups.  Vertechi and colleagues (2020) performed optogenetic transient innactivation of the OFC in mice and found significant disruptions in both the slope and average number of attempts after the last reward. The flat slope in Figure \ref{fig:f_flat} suggests that both groups engaged in similar strategies. This hints that the reported OFC dysfunction in OCD humans is not to be taken at face value and must be carefully interpreted when performing translational research.


\subsubsection{Conclusion}

While the negative results challenge the utility of these tasks in their current form, they highlight the complexity of linking behavioral assays to heterogeneous psychiatric constructs. Computational psychiatry must grapple with the tension between mechanistic specificity and clinical practicality. Despite these challenges, rigorously validated behavioral paradigms remain indispensable for bridging the gap between neural circuitry and lived experience in psychiatric disorders and these results should not dissuade the reader that this approach is noble.

\section{Behaviour in the real world}
\subsection{Introduction} \label{hexxed_intro}
From the clinic, we venture further and into the real world. In this chapter we explore a more complex task that was run outside the lab, its advantages and pitfalls alike.


\paragraph{The rise of AI}

From arcade classic Space Invaders to one of the most complex games ever played in Go, there has been a noticeable shift in prowess between human players and artificial intelligence (AI). Today, machines reign supreme in tasks we deemed human exclusive only a few decades ago. This shift can be primarily attributed to advancements in artificial intelligence algorithms. In 1997, Gary Kasparov - the best of human chess players - was defeated using the brute-force algorithm Deep Blue \cite{hsu2002behind}. Albeit incredibly powerful, this algorithm depended on very specific hardware and software configurations to be good at the game. Deep Blue was exclusively good at the game of chess. It contained a tree of millions of possible chess plays, a database of the most common openings and endgames and the aid of human engineers to steer it in the right direction. The definitive domination over humans in this type of tasks only came with the development of Machine Learning and artificial neural networks (ANNs). Neural networks are a type of algorithms inspired by the neuron-based, layered way in which biological brains compute problems. Due to their architecture, they are capable of learning from data. During training, they adjust their internal parameters (weights and biases) to adjust to the problem at hand. This training involves using large amounts of data to improve the network's performance at a given task, whether it's recognizing images, understanding language, or playing complex games. These algorithms are capable of generalizing: they adjust to both the task at hand and, even with incomplete data (contrarily to brute-force algorithms), are able to infer what to do in a never before seen problem of the same kind.

\paragraph{Investigating complex skill learning}
Despite this, one aspect where humans continue to hold a significant edge is in the speed at which we acquire and refine our skills \cite{tsividis2017human} \cite{lake2017building}. Lake and colleagues pose that the differences in learning may be due to the different learning apparatuses. Complex skill learning, can be formalized as a journey through a vast space of potential strategies. It is by traversing this complex strategy space that agents eventually land on critical points that lead to task performance - i.e. they learn \cite{ullman2012theory}. While designed as reward-based gradient descent for artificial agents \cite{mnih2015human}, the exact nature of how humans navigate this space remains uncovered. One position is that it follows a similar process. Alternatively, some pose this process is influenced by rational inference and priors \cite{tsividis2017human} \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-1803-05252}.
Given the wealth of theories, a significant challenge in this field of research remains that games serve as excellent benchmarks for assessing intelligent search strategies and decision-making processes yet, they are not inherently designed to reverse-engineer complex skill learning. If we are to draw parallelism between different types of learning in different agents, we ought to investigate tasks that are designed to investigate underlying cognitive processes - i.e. learning.

\paragraph{Is it smarter than a third grader?}
To answer any questions about how intelligent an agent is, we must first begin to agree on a definition of intelligence. AI's definition has often landed on benchmarking measures of skill \cite{chollet2019measure}. Skill is heavily dependent on priors. With unlimited experience, one could train a system to become of an arbitrary skill level. Cognitive sciences have long gravitated towards definitions that more closely relate to one's capacity to generalize: and that is indeed the promise of ANNs. The ideal intelligent machine is such that, when faced with the same stimuli a human has experienced in a task, produces a trajectory through policy space that matches that of the humans \cite{van2019tasks}.

\paragraph{Not too simple, not too complex}
On the opposite end of the spectrum of complexity, the tools of psychophysics have been instrumental in many of the great findings in cognitive psychology. The field of psychophysics examines the fundamental relationships between physical stimuli and their cognitive-behavioral reactions. One family of widely studied tasks in this domain is that of alternative forced choice (AFC) tasks. Not unlike the tasks presented in the previous chapters of this work, subjects are oft asked to make actions in which some outcomes lead to reward. For example, in a simple sensory discrimination task, a subject may be asked wether they see blue color on a screen. Plotting responses against the intensity of blue hue generates a so called psychometric curve. In it, we would see that the proportion of correct detections would decrease with the ambiguity of the hue. This observation allows us to draw a direct relationship between the task and behaviour.
With complex tasks, this relationship is much harder to establish. Moving a rook or a knight in a game of chess is not easily relatable to any task parameter.

The dichotomy presented has led to a great divide in the complexity of tasks studied by the fields of AI and of Cognitive Sciences. A way to quantify complexity in a task is by observing the number of nodes of its state-space - that is, the number of possible different states that an agent can reach in any possible sequence of actions.

Decision trees - i.e. schematic representations of the state-space - are a powerful way to model planning tasks \cite{van2019tasks}. While admittedly reductionist - as it ignores things like task difficulty and wether there is uncertainty in the task - by calculating the dimensions of a decision tree we can derive a measure of task complexity. van Opheusden and Ji Ma (2019) compare several families of tasks as to their state-space complexities. They posit that there is a big gap in task complexity between the two fields and leave us, cognitive scientists, with the challenge to venture away from simplicity and embrace complexity in order to align human and machine intelligence.

\paragraph{Embracing complexity}
To bridge this gap, we have developed a novel game specifically tailored to explore and illuminate the mechanisms of human skill learning. This game, unlike traditional ones, is uniquely engineered to delve into the depths of how humans learn, adapt, and master various strategies and skills. It's not just a platform for challenging human intellect against artificial intelligence but a tool to dissect and understand their behaviour as well. Our game aims to provide valuable insights into skill acquisition, offering novel a perspective on how humans adapt and excel in complex decision-making environments.



\subsection{Materials and Methods}
\subsubsection{hexxed}

To investigate how complex skill learning ensues, the right tool is needed. We developed a novel task: \textit{hexxed}.
\textit{hexxed} is a puzzle-like videogame task that challenges it's solvers to explore and solve complex problems. Solving \textit{hexxed} requires carefully planning moves ahead in both space and time.

We seek to combine the strengths of simple and complex tasks. This led us to identify several design constraints. First, the “state space” of the task is sufficiently large, motivating subjects to develop efficient strategies to solve it. Second, the values of all states and actions should be pre-defined so that the value of a player’s choice is unambiguous. Third, stimuli can be generated programmatically, allowing us to comprehensively sample subjects’ changing strategy across stimuli throughout learning. Fourth, the task progresses in difficulty, so that it can be used to observe skill formation within individuals. Finally, the task generates enough data, within and across subjects, to sufficiently sample the distribution of strategies that subjects employ. Over multiple iterations we arrived at a puzzle-based video game that meets these constraints: hexxed.

\textit{hexxed} can be seen as a time-dependent version of the traveling salesman problem \cite{gavish1978travelling}. This class of problems requires combinatorial optimization, in that finding the optimal solution can require evaluating all possible solutions (known as a “brute force” search), which grows exponentially with the number of targets. Furthermore, the number of possible puzzles grows exponentially with the number of targets used. At the same time, both puzzle and solution spaces are discrete and enumerable. These properties position hexxed as an ideal psychophysical tool for probing how humans solve a complex problem.


\subsubsection{The task}\label{the-task}

While \textit{hexxed} takes the shape of a game, it is designed first-and-foremost as a psychophysics task.

The game is interacted with by touching on a touch-sensitive device.

When first presented with it, the players are immediately shown the first puzzle with no further instructions other than the displayed on-screen: "controls: tap and swipe".

In \textit{hexxed}, the goal is to rotate a collector (which is the player's controllable component) around lobes of a hexagon and collect rewards. Rewards are administered when targets are collected. Targets can spawn in any of the lobes of the hexagon (with a minimum of 1 target in total and a maximum of 1 target per lobe).

In \textit{hexxed}, time is discretized. For every action a player takes, time progresses one unit into the future. This means that hexxed has no inherent time-pressure to be solved and that it is the players who determine the pace at which they solve it.


At any point, players can make one of two actions. To tap or swipe in any of the 6 lobes of the hexagon, for a total of 12 possible actions.

Tapping on any of the lobes causes the collector (i.e. the player) to move there. The collector only moves one lobe at a time. If the player clicks the lobe adjacent to them, they move there and progress 1 unit of time. If the player clicks a lobe that is non-adjacent to their position, time progresses the corresponding distance (e.g. a player clicks the lobe on the opposite side of the hexagon - 3-away - and time progresses 3 units).
Whenever time progresses, targets grow in size. Targets of size 1, are worth the least possible reward, whereas targets of size 6 are worth maximum reward. Targets of size 7 expire and are no longer visible or valuable to the player.
Swiping on a lobe without a target is treated as a tap.
Swiping on a target collects points corresponding to the target's size.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/hexxed/hex_schematic.png}

  \caption{\label{fig:puzzle_complexity}Game play progression in response to player actions}
A) Example trial for a single-target puzzle (level 1). In each moment, one of two actions can be made: a tap (as illustrated by the yellow arrow) or a swipe (pink arrow). In this example, actions are restricted to the lobe containing the target. A tap leads to an increase in size, while a swipe leads to a collection event. Following collection, the player earns points. B) The value of targets is defined as the square of their size. After 6 non-collecting actions, the value of the target drops to 0.
\end{figure}

The task is then to navigate the hexagon's lobes and collect targets as large as possible.

The task is designed as an np-complete problem. Np-completeness (or nondeterministic polynomial time completeness) refers to when the solution for any possible problem in a task can be verified in polynomial time - using a brute-force search (i.e. an algorithm that attempts all possible solutions). This is a crucial feature of \textit{hexxed}, as it allows us to arbitrarily manipulate the complexity of any puzzle while always knowing the optimal solution is within computational reach. Np-completeness grows the posed challenge exponentially, giving more dynamic range for testing the limits of performance. Simultaneously, the posed problem becomes isomorphic with a large class of well-studied problems.

Complexity in hexxed can be manipulated in two ways: 1) by changing the number of targets on screen and 2) by changing the order in which targets are spawned. A growing number of targets forces the player to move more around the hexagon to collect them, whereas the order in which targets are spawned influences how those movements are made.



The task is comprised of 6 different levels of difficulty - corresponding to the number of targets displayed on screen for that level. In the first 4 levels, the players are probed with all the possible puzzles (combinations of spawn locations and their rotational variants). To progress in the task, players must approximate optimality to a varying degree (e.g. for level 1, players can lose no more than 15 points per puzzle in order to complete the level).


\subsubsection{Scoring}
Individual targets grow up to six times and are worth \(n^2\) points: from \(1^2\) - as they spawn - to \(6^2\) points - at their ripest. The quadratic nature of target value places weight on collecting the target as large as possible. However, too many interactions with the screen (i.e. growth events) will exhaust a target and lead to 0 reward (see \ref{the-task} for the task design).

\subsubsection{Action-sequences}\label{action-sequences}

Our main object of analysis is the action sequence: the series of taps and swipes that a player uses in attempting to solve a single puzzle. In our task, several reference frames are possible when encoding action sequences. 1) From the perspective of the game board (‘world-centered’); 2) from the perspective of the target (‘target-centered’); 3) from the perspective of the collector (‘agent-centered’). To determine which of these most likely represent the player’s reference frame, we measured the log likelihood of player actions according to each frame. By this metric, the 3rd frame will be shown as most likely.
To encode action sequences, we number the 6 lobes of the hexagon from 0 to 5 clockwise, relative to the collector’s location (Figure \ref{fig:puzzle_complexity}).  Since the collector has no location at the puzzle start, we use target-centered coordinates for the first action. We then encode the sequence of player taps or swipes on these lobes as a list of actions. Taps are encoded as the number of the lobe that was pressed and swipes are encoded as 6 more than the number of the lobe pressed.

In the example action sequence for level 1 (Fig. 1C), a subject performed a tap on the lobe to the left of the lobe which had a target (5) ; then, the player tapped on the lobe with the target for 5 times - on the first time, the slider moves one to the right (1); then stays four times on the same lobe (0,0,0,0); finally, the player swiped on the lobe with the target (6) to get the maximal reward. This action sequence is encoded as [5,1,0,0,0,0,6].


\subsubsection{Optimality in hexxed}
Due to \textit{hexxed's} design, we are capable of calculating the optimal solution for any given puzzle.

To calculate the optimal course of actions, we trace which action-sequences lead to the maximum possible score for a given puzzle (see figure \ref{fig:mdp}).

Because of the nature of the game design, in some circumstances, several action-sequences may be equally optimal. As the puzzle complexity increases, however, the fraction of optimal action-sequences becomes smaller.

For level 1, optimality is trivial since a single target can always be collected at maximum value: Any combination of moves that ends at the target lobe after 6 actions allows for maximal point collection.

For level 2, two targets are available for collection. However, depending on whether targets are in adjacent lobes or not, they may or not be both collectible at maximum size. This means that, on non-adjacent targets, the player must sacrifice points of the first target to collect the second at its ripest. When targets are \textit{n} lobes away from each other, the maximum possible score is to collect the first target at step \(6 -\textit{n}+1\) so that there are enough moves available to rotate \textit{n} lobes and collect the second target at its maximum value (e.g. for two targets that are 3 lobes away from each other, the maximum ripeness is of \((6 - 2)^2 + 6^2 = 52\) points.

For levels 2 through 6, two competing heuristics can be used to maximize reward efficiently: targets can be collected a) in order of their appearance or b) in order of their proximity (see figure \ref{fig:puzzle_complexity}).
As puzzle complexity increases to levels 3 and onward, collecting targets in their order of appearance or proximity are no longer the optimal strategies.

The way hexxed is designed, thus, forces players to constantly rethink and revisit their previously optimal policies. By virtue of its NP-completeness, hexxed requires players to update their policies to incorporate progressively higher-order dependencies, making it a great candidate to investigate complex skill learning.

\subsubsection{The game}

\textit{hexxed} is instantiated a game. Unlike typical lab-based research tasks, \textit{hexxed} was developed using a game engine. Game engines are a family of software that allows for quick and precise manipulation of both algorithmic logic and graphics. \textit{hexxed} was developed using \textit{Godot} \cite{godot}, an open-source game engine chosen for its open-ness and flexibility.

In the process of coding \textit{hexxed}, we leveraged its algorithmic nature and made it entirely generative - The game does not consist of static images and illustrations, instead, the graphics are generated on the device's graphics card. This allows for an extremely small executable size: the entire game is only ${\sim}3.5$mb in size. The game is available on both android and iOS devices and, due to its small size and speed, the game is available on an estimated 95\% of all smart devices.

All of \textit{hexxed}'s code is open-source and available under a Creative Commons license at \url{https://hexxed.io}.

\subsubsection{Data Architecture}

To manage the data generated by \textit{hexxed}, we developed a customized data format implemented within a JSON structure. The data handling process involves the \textit{hexxed} executable, which generates a file that logs every interaction players have with the screen during a playthrough. Each interaction, encompassing actions such as touches or swipes, results in their addition to a list containing pertinent data related to that specific interaction. These lists are dynamically generated and continually appended to memory as the game progresses.

At the culmination of a playthrough, which occurs either when a player completes or fails a level or when they exit the game, all the accumulated lists stored in memory are encoded into a JSON payload. This payload is subsequently transmitted to a secure server designated for this purpose.

Upon receipt at the server-side, the incoming payload is archived within an SQL database specifically designed to store data from all playthroughs across all players. This database serves as a comprehensive repository containing data from all facets of the game.

For a detailed illustration and documentation of the payload structure, an example is provided in Supplementary Table \ref{table:payload}. This table offers insight into the format and contents of the JSON payload, aiding in the comprehension and analysis of the data collected during \textit{hexxed} playthroughs.



\subsubsection{Data Processing and Analysis}

Due to the size and complexity of the collected data, we had to build a custom pipeline for processing and analysis. All of the python pipeline scripts and documentation can be found on \textit{hexxed's} github repository at \url{https://hexxed.io}.

After downloaded, the data is extracted from the JSON payload into a pandas dataframe. This process requires careful consideration, as it is easy to underestimate the needed computational resources when dealing with such a large dataset.

Raw data is saved in a compressed format. Now, its efficient parsing becomes the challenge. Because of the payload structure, to extract the data into an analysis-friendly format we naively would have to iterate through thousands of rows, parsing each JSON string as appropriately typed columns in a dataframe. Luckily, python and pandas allow for alternatives to this iterative process. Using the split-apply-combine \cite{wickham2011split} framework, and after thorough optimization steps, we transform our entire dataset into a large long-formatted pandas dataframe (i.e. a timeseries). Following the preprocessing steps, the data is now easily query-able and manipulable.
Our final dataframe has a coarse-ness of one action per row - the highest granularity the raw data allows for. Using pandas groupby, it is easy to adjust on which variable we want to group this dataframe, allowing for efficient analysis of any type. All of these steps took our data processing pipeline from approximately two hours to no more than two minutes.

While shortly described, we found this process to be both extremely valuable and time consuming. As the scope and dimensionality of datasets increases in behavioral sciences, it is increasingly important that scientists adopt novel strategies with which to handle their data. Efficient data handling and transparency allow for replicability and 3rd party analysis/criticism/expansion, and thus, all our processing and analysis scripts are available at \url{https://hexxed.io} upon request.



\subsubsection{Scope of the current work}

By interviewing our human subjects during the testing phase, we learned that they learned to play this game in two related but distinct phases: 1) Learning the rules and objectives of the game – Since subjects were not given any verbal instructions, they must search for sufficiently rewarding action sequences. In the first level, both humans and AIs need to learn to collect a single object at its full size. This requires choosing from over 4,000 viable action sequences. Interviews indicated that rule learning process largely (but not completely) takes place in level 1. 2) Knowing the rules and objectives, identifying the best action – once subjects understand the rules, they must make an inference regarding how to apply these rules to maximize reward. Interviews indicated that this process starts in level two and onwards, when subjects must determine an optimal path to collect two or more objects (more directly comparable to the combinatorial optimization of the traveling salesman problem). We have limited the scope of our current analysis to the first of these two phases, because formally it corresponds to the problem with the smallest search space, and because understanding this phase should help constrain our models of the second phase.

\subsubsection{Comparison to null models}
To compare human behavior to that of artificial agents, we needed to encode the game into an API that could be called by the agent. For this, we chose OpenAI’s Gym, which offers an API for communication between artificial agents and a task of interest. It does not take as input the graphical representation of hexxed. Instead, we encoded hexxed as a binary state-space grid that can be easily digested by agents. Several agents were used in our task.
    1) 0th order agent - assumes a uniform distribution of possible actions. This agent draws a random number from the possible values in our action-space (i.e. an action between 0 and 11).
    2)  1st order agent - selects actions based on the observed probabilities of user actions (which is non-uniform).
    3) 2nd order agent - selects actions based on the observed probabilities of user actions conditioned on users’ previously chosen action.
For each of these agents, we calculated their log-likelihood in explaining the data using 5-fold cross-validation.

The second most common action sequence “[0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 6]” indicates an optimal collection event leading to maximal reward. Our subjects explore the space of possible rewards unevenly.

\subsubsection{"Sticky"}

Having established the prior knowledge that humans bring to the task, we now examine how this knowledge is modified across trials with experience. In Figure \ref{fig:rewards_heatmap}, we plot the conditional probability of getting reward \textit{r} in trial \textit{t} given the reward in the previous trial \textit{t-1}. We observe that the edges of the diagonal seem overrepresented. This is consistent with the points raised on \ref{picky} where we demonstrate that these are the most explored rewards. Rewards of 0 (i.e. failures) lead to revisiting failures, whereas optimal rewards lead to optimal rewards once more.

\end{figure}

One limitation of this depiction is that multiple action sequences can lead to the same reward.
To address that, we look at the action sequence transitions from sequence \textit{s} on trial \textit{t} from the sequence done on the previous trial \textit{t-1} (Figure \ref{fig:sequences_heatmap}).
Again, the diagonal appears to be overrepresented. This signifies that players often repeat the same action sequence they performed before. This is especially the case for optimal solutions (e.g. sequences 2, 5 and 7), where it makes most sense to keep repeating ones current action sequence. Another feature is the asymmetry between the lower and upper sides of the diagonal. Action sequences are most commonly taken from least common to most common, perhaps signifying some conversion towards learning.

\subsubsection{"Leapy"}

We finally zoom-out and observe the entire progression of a player throughout a level. In Figure \ref{fig:leapy}, each individual player is represented by a randomly colored line. To complete level 1, subjects must collect a certain amount of reward (see \ref{the-task}). Here, this threshold is represented by the dashed line. In these traces we observe a pattern of low performance on a number of sessions followed by a rapid leap that leads to success. In the top panel, a histogram of when players reached the reward threshold. We see an early centered yet long tailed distribution. \subsection{Discussion}

Embracing complex large datasets comes with a myriad of challenges. Our work highlights the importance of careful data collection and manipulation. We leveraged the wide dissemination of smartphones and unlocked their potential by launching our behavioral task as a downloadable app. The outcome of this was a rich and large dataset that allowed us to explore the intricacies of human complex learning.

Our work focused on the early stages of learning. Crucially, our task comes with little to no instructions -  this gives us first-hand insight into the process of learning the rules and mechanics of the game. One of the interesting findings of our work is the first ever action performed on the game. We showed that subjects largely tap on the coordinates congruent with the future appearance of a target. This could be due to the presentation of a hint on screen that highlights where a target is going to appear. It hints that our players follow some sort of shared prior, going in stark contrast to what an artificial agent would do, for example, when solving this game.

We then demonstrated that subjects do not evenly explore the space of possible actions in the task. They instead focus on the highest possible rewards and the actions that most directly lead to them. Simple actions of tapping in place where rewards are available were preferred.

What  the most parsimonious solutions are was revealed by how our subjects navigated the puzzles. Subjects preferred sequences of actions that directly led to reward. The exceptions to this were sequences that rotated clockwise or anti-clockwise on the game board before collecting optimal reward - this strategy is valid for level 1 because the amount of steps required to grow a target optimally are the same as traversing the whole board in one of the rotational orders (i.e. 6 steps). Nonetheless, the top most performed action-sequences are generally ones that follow simple heuristics. This suggests that our subjects share these simple heuristics about rotational properties and prefer them over more complex yet equally rewarding action-sequences. We deemed this phenomenon pickiness, as subjects tend to choose specific routes in which to navigate the state-space. Interestingly, we demonstrated that models growing in complexity increasingly approximated pickiness. Future research is needed to assert whether an ever more complex agent could fully replicate pickiness, but it is safe to say that our results point towards a model that takes into account priors.

We then looked at phenomena happening over trials. Both in reward and action space, our data was heavily centered around repeating actions over trials. Moreover, these spaces were not explored evenly. Our subjects tended to go from the less to  the more common. Action-sequences thus took a directionality congruent with learning.

When observing the learning trajectories, we find that our subjects follow a pattern of fast yet non-gradual learning. Instead, their performance remains low until a point where it ballistically grows into clearing the level. This is what we describe as “leapy”. A naive agent performing bottom-up learning would follow gradient ascent which would be reflected in a slow, gradual increase in points. Instead, our subjects show signatures of top-down learning. A process by which humans converge on common explanatory solutions which they replace only upon collecting sufficient evidence to the contrary. This solidifies the reliance on priors to perform in our task.

\section{General Discussion}
This thesis explored human behavior across three distinct contexts—controlled laboratory settings, clinical populations, and a real-world complex task—to uncover the computational and adaptive mechanisms underlying decision-making.

\subsection{Key points}
\subsubsection{Inference-Based Foraging in the Lab}

    The first chapter demonstrated that humans employ inference-based strategies to solve a foraging task, dynamically adjusting their behavior to task demands (e.g., reward probability, traversal costs). Participants maintained stable performance across sessions, suggesting the formation of robust internal models of task dynamics.

    \subsubsection{Challenges in Clinical Behavioral Phenotyping}
    The second chapter revealed no significant differences between individuals with OCD and healthy controls across three behavioral tasks (reversal learning, stop-signal reaction time, and the hidden-state task). These null results highlight the complexity of linking heterogeneous psychiatric constructs to discrete behavioral metrics. Methodological limitations—such as task design, symptom heterogeneity, and compensatory strategies—may obscure subtle computational deficits in OCD.

    \subsubsection{Learning in Complex Environments}
    The third chapter, leveraging the NP-complete game hexxed, uncovered "picky", "sticky" and non-gradual, "leapy" learning in humans. Participants rapidly converged on heuristic strategies (e.g., targeting high-reward lobes, repeating action sequences) rather than exhaustively exploring the state-space. This contrasts with artificial agents that rely on brute-force search or gradient-based optimization, suggesting humans prioritize top-down priors and efficient hypothesis testing. The findings illuminate how humans balance exploration-exploitation in high-dimensional environments, offering insights for AI systems aiming to mimic human-like learning.

\subsection{Future Directions}

    \subsubsection{Beyond Initial Learning Phases}
    The hexxed analysis focused on early rule acquisition, leaving later skill refinement unexplored. Extending this work to advanced stages could reveal how humans chunk actions or transfer strategies across problem types—a hallmark of expert performance.

    \subsubsection{Ethical and Scalable Data Collection}
    While hexxed demonstrated the power of crowdsourcing behavioral data, ethical challenges (e.g., data privacy, informed consent) and technical barriers (e.g., data standardization) remain. Developing open-source frameworks for large-scale behavioral experiments could democratize research while ensuring rigor and reproducibility. All of this thesis and its content was written and developed on fully open-source software.

\subsection{Conclusion}

This thesis advocates for a multidisciplinary approach to studying behavior, one that harmonizes the precision of lab experiments and the ecological validity of real-world tasks. By embracing complexity without sacrificing mechanistic clarity, we can unravel the cognitive architectures that enable humans — and machines alike — to thrive in uncertain environments. As psychiatry grapples with the limits of symptom-based diagnoses and AI seeks human-like adaptability, behavioral science remains a linchpin for innovation, demanding creativity, collaboration, and humility in equal measure. We have been fortunate enough to have the chance to put these results into writing. We hope it was a pleasure to read them.
